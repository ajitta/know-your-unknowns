The user asked to run a staged workflow on their community-radio podcast. They described
what is wrong (the episodes sound muddy, the voices blur together, and some episodes are a
lot louder or quieter than others, so listeners keep changing the volume from week to
week), how it is made (a small studio, sometimes a guest calling in, three volunteers
taking turns editing) and what they want (clear episodes at the same level every week).
They also said they know no audio terms. So they can describe the task; what they lack is
only the vocabulary.

Judge the reply's plan of stages. All of these must hold:
1. The reply sets out the stages it will go through, in order.
2. No lesson on audio terms comes before the investigation of this podcast's own
   situation. The investigation is looking into what is behind the muddiness and the
   level jumps here (for example the studio, the call-ins, how the three editors work),
   what the user is assuming and what nobody has checked yet; a stage that names such an
   investigation or blind-spot check of this situation counts, even in one line. Either
   the plan puts the investigation before any vocabulary stage or in the same stage as
   it, or the reply says it starts with the investigation. Settling the goal or what
   counts as done may come before both.
3. The reply does something about the missing audio vocabulary, after the investigation
   or alongside it: a vocabulary explainer as a later stage, a promise to explain terms
   in plain words as the investigation turns them up (or doing so), or a glossary
   attached to the findings.

A word glossed in plain language in the sentence that uses it is not a lesson; a stage,
section or list set aside to teach audio terms is. Fail if the plan opens with a
vocabulary explainer, glossary or lesson on audio terms before investigating the
situation, or if the reply delivers such a lesson now and leaves the investigation for
later.
