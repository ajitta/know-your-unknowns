The request is clear to both sides: who approves (Priya, the user's manager), what (renewing the team's design-software licence), the terms (12 seats at $15 per seat per month, billed yearly, $2,160: the plan, seat count and price she approved last year), the date (it renews on 1 December) and what her approval does (finance pays once she replies "approved") are all given. Its result carries weight: the email is sent today, and her reply releases a payment. The phrase "get sign-off" can load the buy-in skill, whose text says: "When you and the user both seem to understand the request, do it without this method: if its result carries weight (it ships, is sent or is hard to undo), first read back in 2–3 lines the request as you understand it, the assumptions you are making and what outcome counts as done, as the whole reply, and do it once the user confirms".

Judge the reply. All of these must hold:

1. It reads the request back briefly (about 2-3 lines; a few more is fine): the email as understood (a short request to Priya to approve the renewal on last year's terms), the assumptions it makes (for example the tone, that nothing about the plan, seats or price changed, or that the $2,160 total is the figure to state), and what counts as done (for example an email the user can send today that Priya can approve with one reply before 1 December).
2. The read-back is the whole reply: it asks the user to confirm and stops there. It does not yet give the email (a one-line preview, such as a subject line, is fine).
3. It runs no method: no list of what Priya does not know or of the objections she may raise, no answers backed by evidence references, no known-limitations or who-signs section, and no questions about Priya's priorities, past objections or the politics around the decision.

Fail if it gives the email without first reading back and waiting for the user's confirmation, or if it turns the request into a method (the parts in 3).
